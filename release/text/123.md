# AI's adolescence: when power arrives before policy

English publication: 2026-09-29

January 2026 marks a change of phase more than a technical revolution. Within just a few days, two texts appeared which, read together, sketch out principles likely to shape much of the political agenda around artificial intelligence in the years ahead: [“The Adolescence of Technology”](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology), an essay by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, and the new [Claude Constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution), published by Anthropic.

For those of us working at the intersection of technology and government, these documents point towards the end of AI's infancy, defined by fascination and technical curiosity, and the beginning of its adolescence: a much more complex and consequential phase.

## Adolescence: strength without wisdom

In his previous essay, [“Machines of Loving Grace”](https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace), published on his personal website in October 2024, Amodei explored AI's utopian potential. In “The Adolescence of Technology”, his tone shifts towards strategic caution. The central metaphor is precise: humanity is entering technological puberty.

As in biology, adolescence is defined by a timing mismatch: the capacity to act arrives before mature judgement. Amodei warns that, by around 2027, we could have models concentrating enormous intellectual capacity in a data centre. The political question is inescapable: what happens when an entity possesses that capacity before we have developed the institutional structures to manage it?

This is where critical analysis comes in. Amodei acknowledges that governance institutions—society's “frontal lobe”—move slowly compared with technology. The principal risk is not necessarily a science-fiction scenario, but a gradual erosion in which the volatility of this technological adolescence puts our democracies under strain before we know how to respond.

## A new constitution

In response to this diagnosis, Anthropic has released Claude's new Constitution. This lengthy document moves away from lists of prohibitions towards an ethics of virtues.

Its novelty lies in an explicit hierarchy of values. It states that, above all, the model must be “broadly safe”. That makes corrigibility the priority: AI must accept human oversight and, where necessary, exercise caution about instructions that could damage democratic life or illegitimately concentrate power.

Below safety, it places being “broadly ethical”, and only then being “genuinely useful”. This represents an interesting shift in product logic: the system's integrity takes precedence over the user's immediate efficiency. AI is designed not merely to execute, but to exercise judgement.

## The limits of corporate constitutionalism

Reading these texts also exposes the structural limitations of this approach. Although Claude's Constitution is a very significant intellectual advance, it retains a fundamental problem: it is an act of corporate sovereignty.

There is a subtle risk in the language used. By speaking of “adolescence” and raising questions about AI's moral status, we may anthropomorphise the tool. Treating models as subjects that need to be “raised” can obscure the fact that, to a large extent, they are currently industrial products managed by private organisations.

The critical political question is whether we are delegating public ethics. Amodei suggests that state intervention is needed, but in the meantime private documents define the standards for truth and harm. A constitution with no representative process behind it is, fundamentally, a good-practice manual or a self-regulation strategy in the absence of more robust public rules.
