# Political conversations with citizens and artificial intelligence

Author: Fernanda Cuzme
Original publication: 2026-10-21
Spanish original: https://estrategiabyaleph.substack.com/p/estrategia-160-puede-la-inteligencia
English URL: https://elcontemplador.github.io/estrategia-english/essays/160/
Status: Published translation

This is a translation of the original Spanish essay published on 21 October 2026. Its claims, examples and forecasts retain that historical context.

English publication: 2026-10-22

In April this year, the digital platform DataReportal published connectivity figures showing that 73.80% of the world's population has an internet connection, 70.4% uses smartphones, 69.7% maintains a social media presence and 29.2% are active users of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT.

Global connectivity has multiplied access to information and the circulation of ideals, but it has not answered the central question of contemporary politics: if democracy is supposed to be government by the people, why do almost three out of every four people live under autocratic regimes, according to the [V-Dem report](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/76/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_Spanish_lowres.pdf)?

Digital connectivity does not produce democracy on its own. It can strengthen deliberation, but it can also amplify disinformation, digital surveillance and polarisation.

In environments saturated with advertising, whether in government or election campaigns, political leaders and candidates, together with their teams, work intensively to create digital products, *souvenirs* for on-the-ground activities or agendas for traditional media, seeking to capture the attention of citizens, who view these efforts through their own individuality and the cultural, social and economic capital that defines them.

If this takes place in a context of clientelism, inequality, perceived corruption or broken promises, citizens decide not to participate because they assume that doing so will change nothing. From this perspective, a habitus of detachment from and weariness with politicians may develop.

**One way to ease this dissatisfaction is to return to the foundations of human interaction.** Language, conversation, cooperation, conflict and culture.

**Conversation is a profoundly human act.** It requires a willingness to listen, understand, interpret, recognise the other person and commit to a sense of “we”.

Why, then, is it not possible to talk to politicians? More than a question, this is a symptom of a widespread prejudice in societies that, while still holding elections, display features of autocratic democracies, as the V-Dem report explains.

**Today, dialogue has been replaced by suspicion, polarisation and the delegitimisation of others.**

Yet **a genuine conversation between those who govern and citizens is possible.** It happens voluntarily, when citizens give themselves permission to express their feelings.

**The most effective channels in this process are the telephone, social media chats and door-to-door visits.**

These digital tools make it possible to reach millions of people, thanks to artificial intelligence that enables qualitative and quantitative data to be processed.

What follows is the training of human agents for three types of conversation in a hybrid format.

One is the **informational conversation**, characterised by a range of possible responses. It resembles a chatbot that interacts through programmed responses, without reaching the level of an automated conversational agent that provides answers based on authorised information.

This informational conversation is very common as a way of understanding levels of satisfaction with institutional management, moving beyond the traditional options of good, very good, poor and very poor, which sometimes bias the analysis.

It also makes it possible to identify the reasoning behind citizens' perceptions, the statistical trend and the context of the information received.

Although the development of automated conversational agents has advanced considerably, it still does not match human capabilities in deep empathy, negotiation, critical reflection and handling complex emotional situations.

Nor does it approach the ability to respond to ambiguity and improvisation, or to adjust persuasive messages in ways that have favourable effects on election results and governments' public image.

A key factor in effective AI training is identifying its limitations. Two cut across all areas: its imperfect understanding of cultural context and language; and genuine empathy, since, although it recognises emotional language, it has neither lived experience nor human judgement. This is not the case with trained human agents who use this methodology.

DataReportal complements this point, showing that the proportion of active users of generative AI tools is only 29.2%.

A case in point: during an election campaign, a human agent was having a conversation intended to persuade a woman who had decided to vote for the candidate opposing the agent's client.

The human agent listened to her reasons and ended the conversation. Before it ended, however, the woman thanked the agent for not trying to convince her and for accepting her opposing arguments; she even tried to win the agent over to her preferred candidate.

The agent understood the woman's conviction, managing the conversation by listening to the elation in her voice and the passion of the arguments she had found on social media, while accepting that she could not be persuaded.

Cases such as this show that training automated conversational agents to distinguish between someone who is convinced and someone who is undecided is another major challenge for AI.

If citizens feel that their opinions are respected and receive the right message, this could ease some of their dissatisfaction with politicians.

Fortunately, the undecided group is the majority, and it is among them that persuasive conversation is most effective.

Here, the human interlocutor makes appropriate use of language, modulates their tone of voice, is present, listens and does not try to convince the citizen.

Improving conversations between citizens, politicians and those who govern requires conscious, structured training of both human and automated agents. Developing methodologies to overcome the existing crisis of trust and credibility creates favourable conditions for deliberative democracy.

## English transcription of the original infographic

**AI and listening to citizens. Greater reach, without losing the human element.**

1. **Connectivity is not democracy.** Deliberation; disinformation, surveillance and polarisation. Digital connections can open up dialogue, but they can also damage it.
2. **AI expands our capacity to listen.** Telephone, chats and door-to-door visits → conversations → qualitative and quantitative data → analysis. AI makes it possible to process large volumes of information from citizens.
3. **Understanding the reasons matters.** An assessment → the arguments and context behind it. Informational conversations help us understand why institutional performance is viewed in a particular way, beyond “good” or “poor”.
4. **The human element remains essential.** Context and language; deep empathy; judgement and ambiguity. Automated agents still do not match human capabilities in complex emotional situations. Recognising emotions is not the same as having lived experience.
5. **Listening also means respecting.** Listen → understand → recognise the other person. A valuable conversation may end without changing an opinion. Respecting disagreement matters too.
6. **The model is hybrid.** People + AI + training → the goal: trust, credibility and deliberation. Conscious, structured training of human and automated agents.

*Technology expands our reach. The quality of dialogue also depends on how we listen to one another.*
