That was the original idea, but **the release of [ChatGPT’s advanced voice model](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/09/24/1104422/openai-released-its-advanced-voice-mode-to-more-people-heres-how-to-get-it/), [long awaited since its presentation in May](https://open.substack.com/pub/estrategiabyaleph/p/estrategia-33-google-vs-openai-una?r=2tyybn&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web), changed our plans.** It is not yet available in the EU, but we have been able to try it from Spain using a VPN. **This voice interface is revolutionary in its naturalness, despite arriving with its capabilities severely curtailed compared with the demonstration:** it still has no vision and does not integrate with GPTs; for safety reasons, it has been “forbidden” to sing, clone voices or recognise different people; and, for the time being, it has been deprived of memory.

Even so, **it is undoubtedly the beginning of a shift towards a new way of communicating with machines, very close to how we speak with another person. As the example below will show, it also offers enormous possibilities for political communication and learning.**

## Testing GPT’s advanced voice mode to prepare for a debate

When the model was presented more than four months ago, it occurred to me that one of the main uses of an AI that could speak like a human—and even interrupt or raise its voice, although in practice this is now much more restricted than the model itself allows—combined with GPT-4o’s reasoning capabilities, would be as **an excellent “sparring partner” for any politician preparing for a debate or a parliamentary speech involving a direct exchange with a colleague from another political camp.**

**The audio we present below is our first take with this model.** Its imperfections are evident: for example, it takes on the role of a government member even though I have asked it to play the opposition. It does, however, correct itself very quickly. **We are presenting it as it is so that you can see how spontaneously the system works.**

We had complex briefing notes from several parties on a range of subjects. But since advanced voice mode cannot yet be integrated with GPTs and has no dedicated long-term memory—two things that will probably change within a few months, allowing us to test the model much more thoroughly, as will the option of voice-specific “custom instructions”—**we preferred to create a simple set of arguments using [Perplexity](https://www.perplexity.ai/search/quiero-que-busques-en-internet-fJRZf5GNQai07uJW2E9.Yw).** We also chose **a subject** that, although very topical in Spain, is general and applicable in any country: **whether or not to reduce working hours.**

**Below are the arguments we gave the AI (the opposition’s) and those we used ourselves (prepared for the government). They are followed by the exact, spontaneous, entirely unedited audio of our first mock “debate”.** Despite being a fairly spontaneous and very preliminary trial—this will improve enormously with prior customised instructions—it demonstrates many of the model’s possibilities:

### Accessible translation of the historical Perplexity briefing

*The following is the AI-generated text shown in the original screenshot, translated into English. It records the material used in this 2024 experiment. The numbered references are reproduced as displayed; their source list is not visible in the capture.*

> Based on the available information, here is a summary of the arguments for and against reducing working hours in Spain:
>
> **Arguments in favour (trade unions and left-wing parties)**
>
> - **Better quality of life:** A shorter working day contributes to better mental and physical health for workers, reducing stress and exhaustion. [5, 6]
> - **Increased productivity:** Several studies suggest that reducing working hours can increase productivity, as workers are more focused and efficient during their actual working time. [5, 6]
> - **Lower unemployment:** Reducing working hours could help distribute employment more widely, allowing more staff to be hired to cover the reduced hours. [5, 7]
> - **Work–life balance:** Employees would have more time for their families, leisure, training or rest, improving the balance between work and personal life. [1, 6]
> - **Social justice:** Trade unions argue that this measure would help distribute the wealth generated by the country more equitably. [1, 2]
> - **Positive impact on the economy and the environment:** Reducing working hours could have a positive effect on the country’s economy and the environment. [1, 2]
>
> **Arguments against (CEOE, employers’ organisations and right-wing parties)**
>
> - **Higher costs for businesses:** Businesses could face higher labour costs if they need to hire more staff to cover the reduced hours or pay overtime. [3, 5]
> - **Loss of competitiveness:** In a globalised market, reducing working hours could affect Spanish businesses’ competitiveness relative to their international competitors. [3, 5]
> - **Impact on specific sectors:** The adverse effects would be greater for smaller businesses and in sectors such as tourism, hospitality and retail, where face-to-face contact with customers is essential. [3]
> - **Higher unit labour costs:** A compulsory reduction in working hours with wages held constant would increase unit labour costs, which could adversely affect businesses’ competitiveness and trends in employment and GDP. [3]
> - **Competitive disadvantages:** Businesses operating in global markets may face competitive disadvantages if their labour costs rise relative to those in countries with longer working hours. [3]
> - **Preference for collective bargaining:** CEOE and Cepyme reject a statutory reduction in working hours and point to collective bargaining as the framework for addressing a reduction in working time. [4]
>
> It is important to emphasise that these arguments represent each group’s general positions and that nuances and variations may exist within each perspective.
