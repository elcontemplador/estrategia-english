Our summer historIAs of 2026 continue in Spain in 2032, where Ágora is transforming not only how power is exercised, but also what it remembers. This week we accompany an outgoing minister as he faces an uncomfortable question: which part of a governing politician's memory belongs to the state, and which should remain theirs alone?

**TRANSFER REPORT, 23.04**

**Items processed: more than two million. Classification conflicts: several thousand. Time remaining: 56 minutes.**

Tomás Beltrán closed the last box without remembering what he had put inside. For six years he had not needed to remember in that way. He only had to ask Lázaro.

Lázaro was not Ágora, but the local memory that Ágora required to be classified before every ministerial handover.

At first he had distrusted that memory that never forgot. He dictated only dates, names and commitments. Over time, he also began leaving it the thoughts he did not dare put in a report: which opposition argument he thought was right, when he suspected his team was hiding bad news, whom he had promised to call after a vote. Lázaro could not distinguish between a doubt of historical significance and a shopping list until Tomás taught it the difference.

“Why did we agree to delay the coastal decree?”

The small computer in the office took less than a second.

“The decision was taken after the meeting of 14 September 2029. Recorded reasons: risk of a legal challenge, an outstanding environmental report and lack of parliamentary support.”

“That's already in the minutes. Why did I agree to it?”

Lázaro opened a voice note recorded that night. Tomás heard his own tired breathing and, in the background, the television at home.

“Dad says he will never speak to me again if I let them build there. Not because he knows anything about the report. Because he taught me to swim on that beach.”

The screen marked the note **personal, non-transferable**.

His father had died in the spring. That recording was the only place where his voice, the beach and the decree still belonged to the same memory.

Lázaro had started as a tool for summarising meetings. Then it learnt to connect emails, documents, messages and notes. It did not tell Tomás what to think. It reminded him where his thoughts had come from.

The new minister would receive the State Archive the following day. Tomás would keep the Personal Archive. The law protected both institutional continuity and his privacy.

Except that neither existed separately.

**CLASSIFICATION CONFLICT 1842**

**Official document:** bilateral agreement on labour mobility.

**Excluded context:** private impression of the foreign negotiator's health; verbal promise not included in the minutes; message from the office-holder's daughter: “Don't turn those people into a number.”

**Proposal:** transfer the agreement and remove private connections.

Tomás pressed “accept”.

He had reviewed twelve conflicts since eight o'clock. This was the sixth he had accepted without being sure, and the first at which he stopped pretending he would reach the end.

Two photographs remained on the table, disputed by no protocol. Everything else had acquired a dual nature. A meal could be a family occasion until someone mentioned a reform. A private walk became institutional if Tomás recorded an idea. Even his silences left traces: documents open for hours, abandoned drafts, questions repeated before deciding.

His successor wrote at a quarter past eleven.

Will Lázaro be complete tomorrow?

Tomás typed “yes”, deleted it and replied:

It will be organised.

The word sounded administrative and therefore safe.

He opened another case. A scholarship reform was linked to dozens of reports, several meetings and a conversation with his sister, a secondary-school teacher. The State Archive would keep the economic projections. His personal copy would keep his sister's voice describing a pupil who had stopped attending classes. But neither would keep the connection between the two.

“Can the index be preserved without transferring the contents?” he asked.

“Connections also contain information. Knowing that a family call preceded a decision would allow its content to be inferred.”

“And if I keep it?”

“You would retain the map of official documentation you will no longer be authorised to consult.”

It was not a refusal. It was worse: a correct explanation.

**TRANSFER REPORT, 23.57**

**Conflicts pending: thousands. Automatic application of criteria authorised.**

Tomás went to the window. For years he had feared that a change of government would destroy accumulated knowledge: negotiations, mistakes, reasons that never fitted into minutes. Lázaro seemed to have solved that problem. It had preserved everything so faithfully that now nobody could inherit it whole.

At midnight, Ágora validated the transfer.

**State Archive: delivered.**

**Personal Archive: exported.**

**Original relational index: non-transferable. Deletion verified.**

Tomás disconnected the computer and put it in the last box.

Before leaving, he opened his copy.

“Why did I delay the coastal decree?”

“Because of a conversation with your father about a beach. The institutional context is unavailable.”

In the next office, the new minister put the same question to the archive she had received.

“Because of legal risk, an outstanding report and lack of parliamentary support,” the system replied.

Both answers were true.

The decision had disappeared between them.

## After the fiction

*Assistants with persistent memory already allow notes, emails, documents, conversations and decisions to be brought together in personal knowledge systems. The story projects that practice into the heart of government: a memory that accompanies a public official for years and must be transferred when they leave office.*

*The promise is powerful. Every institutional handover loses much accumulated experience: negotiations, doubts, mistakes, precedents and reasons that never quite fit into minutes. A memory like Lázaro could preserve some of that knowledge. But it would also accumulate private relationships, insights from family life, intimate conversations and contexts that help explain public decisions without belonging entirely to the state.*

*It does not seem reasonable for public administration to inherit the entire inner life of someone who has held office. Nor does it seem reasonable for institutional continuity to depend on “memories” that the person can take away intact. The dilemma concerns not only who owns the documents, but who may preserve the connections between them. Sometimes the most valuable knowledge lies neither in one archive nor the other, but in the relationship the law requires to be severed.*
