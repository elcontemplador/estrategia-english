How can you work with AI without giving up thinking for yourself?

Andrés Ferraro Sastre explores how to use AI to ask better questions, preserve independent judgement and distinguish faster work from better work.

Andrés Ferraro SastreAuthor
4 November 2026Originally published in Spanish
5 min readEnglish edition · 5 November 2026

There is a scene that is beginning to recur in many companies: someone needs to prepare a report. They open an artificial intelligence tool, type in some instructions and, within seconds, have a well-organised, clear and seemingly correct text.

They read it.

It looks good to them.

They use it.

And carry on working.

Everything has happened much faster than before.

But there is a question that seems increasingly important to me: have we worked better, or have we simply worked faster? The difference matters.

After years of working with companies, executives and teams, I have learnt that technology can change processes, but real change happens when people change the way they think, decide and work.

And artificial intelligence brings us face to face with precisely that challenge: the temptation to let the machine do the thinking.

Let us agree that AI is an extraordinarily useful tool, of course. It can help us organise information, summarise documents, generate ideas, prepare drafts, analyse data or suggest alternatives.

It allows us to complete in minutes tasks that might previously have taken hours.

That is productivity, and it would be absurd not to make use of it.

The problem arises when we confuse saving time with no longer thinking, because a well-written answer can be wrong.

Or a brilliant idea may not be useful for our problem.

Or an apparently complete analysis may be based on insufficient information, may it not?

A text that sounds convincing may also fail to express what we really want to say… and so on, endlessly, or almost.

AI can produce an answer quickly, but it cannot take responsibility on our behalf for the consequences of using it.

That responsibility remains ours.

The art of asking ourselves questions…¶

When I use artificial intelligence, there is one question I find particularly useful:

Can I delegate this, or do I need to understand it?

I can ask a tool to organise a document.

I can ask it to turn some ideas into a presentation.

I can request different alternatives for solving a problem.

But if I have to make an important decision, I need to understand the problem.

I need to know the context, and I need to question the answers.

And, ultimately, I need to decide for myself. Full stop.

Of course, it sounds simple. Yet the more convenient technology becomes, the easier it is to stop making that effort.

And that is precisely where one of the risks I consider most important emerges: I am not only concerned that AI might get things wrong.

I am concerned that we might get used to not checking it. Do you see what I mean? It is not the tool; it is what happens to us and how we respond to what happens when we engage with it.

That is why questions take centre stage again¶

For years, we have placed great value on the ability to find answers.

That is why I believe one of our key skills in this new phase will be learning to ask better questions.

Asking good questions forces us to think.

What problem are we trying to solve?

What information do we really need?

What are we taking for granted?

What are we overlooking?

What could go wrong?

What other perspective should we consider?

When we use AI in this way, it stops being merely a tool for producing content and becomes a tool for stimulating our own thinking.

And that, for me, is where a truly “intelligent” use of technology begins.

Even now that Donald Trump has decided officially to rename AI as SI (superintelligence) within the US administration.

Whatever we call it, I would like us to begin using AI, or whatever its name may be, as a mirror. Could we do that?

We could put an idea to it and ask it to find weaknesses, to play devil's advocate, or something along those lines…

We could show it a proposal and ask it to raise objections, or put an investment to it and ask it to look for the “dark side” of that.

And that is precisely where our relationship with technology begins to change.

We are asking it to help us think better. There lies the difference. There is our humanity working with technology “in real time”.

In our day-to-day work at HumanIA Consulting, we see something of this happening in companies. There is little point in introducing AI tools if people do not know what problems they want to solve, what decisions they need to make or what results they are trying to achieve.

It is a matter of purpose and organisational culture. That is the real transformation. The genuine kind, not the one posted on LinkedIn to make things look good.

That is why I believe the real value still lies in people. It is easy to say, but we will have to put it into practice ourselves.

We are entering a fascinating phase, in which the most profound changes in history are passing through our fingers…

AI is going to transform many tasks. Some will disappear, others will change, and new ways of working will emerge.

But some capabilities will remain essential: interpreting, questioning, listening, understanding context, taking responsibility and making decisions.

Here is to more conversations and more active listening.

To more questions among ourselves and our teams.

Of course, there are no manuals. Learning happens day by day, among us.

Let us keep learning, together.

Andrés Ferraro Sastre

CEO of HumanIA Consulting, executive coach and communications expert.

English transcription of the original infographic¶

Working with AI without giving up thinking.

Greater speed ≠ better judgement. A convincing answer can also be wrong.

What should you delegate, and what should you understand?

Draw on AI:

  • Organise information.
  • Prepare drafts.
  • Explore alternatives.

Retain your own judgement:

  • Understand the context.
  • Check answers.
  • Make decisions.

Use AI as a mirror. Ask it for objections, weaknesses and other perspectives.

“What am I taking for granted?” “What am I overlooking?” “What could go wrong?”

Purpose first. The tool comes afterwards. Define the problem and the result you are looking for.

Delegating tasks does not mean delegating responsibility.
