We test ChatGPT Images 2.0: a comparison with Nano Banana Pro and new uses for government, politics and public administration

An April 2026 comparison of image models through public communication experiments, with the original prompts and generated examples.

Fernando Nieto LobatoAuthor
29 April 2026Originally published in Spanish
24 min readEnglish edition · 29 September 2026

Archive note: this article reports experiments published on 29 April 2026. Model availability, rankings and expectations retain that date. The generated posters, statistics and procedural advice below are historical demonstrations, not verified official information. English transcriptions reproduce their visible content, including anomalies.

Just a few days ago, on 21 April, OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Images 2.0, its new image generation and editing model integrated into ChatGPT. The tool can already be used within the conversation itself, including on free accounts, although with usage limits and some advanced functions reserved for paid plans. As usually happens with these launches, it is wise to keep a little distance from the initial enthusiasm, but our first tests point to a clear improvement: greater visual coherence, a better understanding of complex instructions, more orderly composition and, above all, an increasingly reliable ability to integrate legible text into images, which makes a real difference for many uses. The first comparisons by Artificial Analysis and Arena AI also place it at the top of image generation, ahead of GPT Image 1.5 (the previous model), Nano Banana 2 and Nano Banana Pro (Google’s models), by a considerable margin.

That is why in this issue we wanted to run a direct test: take the vast majority of the prompts we used in estrategIA #115 — “Ten strategic uses of Nano Banana Pro for communicating public policies, campaigns and services”, published on 10 December 2025 — and run them again with ChatGPT Images 2.0. The aim is not to decide which model “draws prettier pictures”, but to see whether this latest leap makes more robust uses possible in political communication, government and public administration. We encourage you to open the earlier issue and compare the results from the two models, bearing in mind that these are the first results produced by each prompt: we neither edited them nor made several attempts in either case. After that comparison, we also bring you several new cases that we think are particularly interesting.

Some uses from the December issue, revisited for comparison¶

All the prompts are in the original article; we will not repeat them and make this newsletter even longer, but they are available there for you to run your own tests and designs. Of the ten uses we presented in December, we have chosen five examples to show below, again for reasons of space. However, we have tested almost all of them, and the new model handles them without difficulty and, in our view, with higher quality; its handling of text stands out in particular.

Five examples of new uses with ChatGPT Images 2.0¶

Since the model, as the benchmarks clearly show, represents a significant leap forward in image generation — we do not think Google will take long to respond with another iteration of its models, probably at its major developer conference, Google I/O, in mid-May — we bring you new possible uses in politics, public administration and government, together with prompts we co-generated with GPT-5.5.

An infographic for a crisis plan¶

Prompt, translated from the Spanish used in the experiment:

“Crisis room: the first six hours of a viral falsehood about a public institution.” The image should represent an institutional coordination centre, not a chaotic scene. Combine a visual scene of a crisis room with a clear operational panel. Include a six-hour timeline: Hour 0: detection of viral content. Hour 1: internal verification. Hour 2: consultation with official sources. Hour 3: response decision. Hour 4: publication of a clarification. Hour 5: impact monitoring. Hour 6: report and learning. Add three side columns: responsible people; critical decisions; mistakes to avoid. Style: serious, institutional, realistic and modern, with the appearance of an operational protocol for a government office. Perfectly legible Spanish text. Do not use the names of parties or real people.

English transcription of the generated crisis plan: “Crisis room: the first six hours of a viral falsehood about a public institution”. Coordination · verification · transparency · responsibility.

Responsible team Role

Communication Drafts messages, prepares clarifications and manages spokespeople.
Verification Cross-checks information, analyses the falsehood and assesses its actual reach.
Legal Assesses legal implications and institutional risks.
Technology/data Monitors networks, analyses metrics and detects trends.
Relevant services Provide technical information and operational support.

Hour Stage Tasks displayed
0 Detect viral content Alert triggered by an unusual increase in mentions; identify the falsehood and make an initial assessment; activate the crisis room immediately.
1 Internal verification Coordination meeting; gather data and background; confirm facts within the institution.
2 Consult official sources Contact relevant departments and bodies; request official information and documents; cross-check data.
3 Response decision Assess response options; approve the key message and spokespeople; communication and channel plan.
4 Publish clarification Disseminate the official message; publish through institutional channels; coordinate with media and allies.
5 Monitor impact Real-time monitoring; analysis of metrics and sentiment; detect new related falsehoods.

Critical decisions: confirm whether the falsehood is false, misleading or out of context; determine actual impact and potential reach; decide the best time to respond; define a clear, truthful and empathetic key message; choose spokespeople and communication channels; maintain constant internal coordination.

Mistakes to avoid: responding without checking information; minimising or ignoring the falsehood; contradictions between departments or spokespeople; technical or defensive language; attacking or disparaging citizens; publishing late or through unsuitable channels; failing to document decisions and lessons.

The central screens show “Network monitoring: 32.7K total mentions”, sources X (Twitter), Facebook, news and others; “Viral falsehood detected — high potential reach — verifying information”; impact and development lines labelled mentions/potential reach; and sentiment values of 62% negative and 24% neutral. A positive category is visible, but its value is obscured. Small source-count labels and graph ticks are not reliably legible. Key messages: transparency, verified information, commitment to truth, service to citizens. The room sign says “Institutional coordination”; the flipchart lists truthfulness, speed, responsibility, empathy and public service.

Footer: “Truthfulness · speed · coordination · empathy · public service. Informing people well, on time and responsibly strengthens public trust.”

A visual kit for a citizens’ assembly, as an example¶

Prompt, translated from Spanish:

Create a visual kit for a citizens’ assembly on “How should our council use artificial intelligence?” The image should look like a deliberative working table viewed from above, with carefully organised printed materials. Include: 1. A card with debate rules: listen; argue respectfully; ask for evidence; distinguish facts from opinions. 2. Three scenario cards: AI for citizen services; AI for internal management; AI for security and emergencies. 3. A risks card: privacy; bias; errors; digital exclusion. 4. An opportunities card: speed; accessibility; time savings; better services. 5. A final sheet: “Questions for deciding”. Style: realistic, warm, institutional and participatory, like materials prepared by a council for a citizens’ event. Brief, legible Spanish text. Format 16:9.

English transcription of the generated kit: “Citizens’ assembly — How should our council use artificial intelligence? We participate today; we decide better.”

1. Debate rules: listen to every opinion without interrupting; argue respectfully, without personal attacks; ask for data and examples before expressing an opinion; distinguish facts from opinions, separating what we know from what we think. “Every voice counts.”

2. Scenario 1 — AI for citizen services: chatbots and assistants 24/7; clear, personalised information; faster replies; accessible channels for everyone. “Which uses would we prioritise? What precautions should we take?”

3. Scenario 2 — AI for internal management: automate repetitive tasks; analyse data to make better decisions; detect incidents and optimise resources; support municipal staff. “Which processes would we improve? Where should we set limits?”

4. Scenario 3 — AI for security and emergencies: early risk detection; more effective coordination; real-time information; prediction and prevention. “Which benefits and precautions matter most?”

5. Risks: privacy — protect personal data and ensure responsible use; bias — avoid discriminatory or unfair algorithmic decisions; errors — AI can fail, so we need human oversight; digital exclusion — nobody should be left behind through lack of access or skills. “Fewer risks, more trust.”

6. Opportunities: speed — faster replies and procedures; accessibility — services that are easier for everyone to use; time savings — less waiting, more time for what matters; better services — data-based decisions to improve life in the city. “Use technology for a fairer and more efficient city.”

7. Questions for deciding: What problems in our municipality could AI help solve? Which AI uses do we consider priorities, and why? Which risks concern us most, and how can we mitigate them? What safeguards and limits must the council observe? What do we need to ensure nobody is left behind?

Additional cards say: “Your participation improves our municipality. Thank you.” “Your opinion builds our city.”

A stakeholder map, in this case for a low-emission zone¶

Prompt, translated from Spanish:

Act as a political communication strategist and stakeholder-map designer. Create a square infographic entitled “Stakeholder map for a new low-emission zone”. Visually represent five groups: 1. Affected residents. 2. Businesses and hospitality. 3. Environmental associations. 4. Regular motorists. 5. Municipal government and opposition. For each group include: likely attitude — support, uncertainty, opposition or negotiation; main concern; recommended message; most useful communication channel. Organise the composition as a map of connected nodes around the central public measure, “Low-emission zone”. Style: institutional, analytical and modern; no caricatures; no partisan propaganda; clear icons; perfectly legible Spanish text; a sober palette, like a strategic report. Format 1:1.

Affected residents Uncertainty Mobility restrictions and possible financial costs; unequal effects across neighbourhoods. Less pollution, better health and quality public spaces for everyone. Residents’ meetings, neighbourhood associations, local social media and the council website.

A rapid briefing format: a decision in five minutes, for advising politicians or public managers¶

Prompt, translated from Spanish:

Act as an adviser in a public-sector cabinet and a strategic information designer. Create a portrait A4 visual briefing entitled “Decision in five minutes: introducing generative AI into municipal citizen services”. The piece should be designed for a local government meeting. Include these clearly differentiated blocks: 1. Public problem. 2. Objective of the measure. 3. Three decision options: limited pilot; gradual deployment; broad deployment. 4. Expected benefits. 5. Administrative risks. 6. Ethical and legal risks. 7. Minimum conditions for approving the measure. 8. Final recommendation. Very important: do not invent figures. If information is missing, use placeholders such as “[data pending]”. Text must be brief, perfectly legible and in Spanish. The design should resemble an executive document from a European public-sector consultancy: sober, clear and elegant, with discreet icons, a strong visual hierarchy and plenty of white space. Portrait A4 format.

1. Public problem: high demand across multiple channels — telephone, website, email, in person and social media; slow and uneven responses depending on channel or time; a heavy administrative burden on municipal staff; information scattered across websites and complex procedures. 2. Objective: provide faster, clearer and more consistent answers 24/7; increase service capacity without expanding the organisational structure; free staff time for higher-value tasks; improve citizens’ experience and trust in the council. Approach: generative AI supports municipal staff rather than replacing them.

3. Decision options:

Analysis of hairstyles or recommended clothing colours¶

One of the more “curious” uses, and one enjoying considerable success online, applies these models and their multimodal capabilities to questions of style and personal appearance. Here are a couple of prompts and examples — this time we have used as our model the AI “candidate” we created for the European elections back in April 2024, in issue 29 of estrategIA. I am not a specialist in this field and do not have sufficient expertise to judge the results in depth. They will probably depend heavily on the reference photograph itself and how it was taken. But this seems an interesting advance on the simpler hairstyle trial we presented in the December issue.

Prompt, translated from Spanish:

Create a hairstyle-analysis graphic using this portrait. Show side-by-side hairstyle comparisons to highlight which ones best suit the person portrayed.

Prioritise the visual element, using only short labels and no paragraphs.

English transcription of the model’s hairstyle analysis, using the fictional candidate: “Find your best version”.

Starting point: heart-shaped face; straight or wavy hair; medium-to-high density. Most flattering: long layers, textured lob, feathered hair. May also work: classic bob, medium-length layers, centre parting. Less recommended: straight cut without layers.

Flattering features: layers that add movement; subtle highlights for brightness; volume through the mid-lengths and ends; a slightly side-set parting. Details that make a difference: soft highlights, face-framing layers, side parting, natural waves and volume at the roots.

Second prompt, translated from Spanish:

Using this portrait, create a personal colour analysis centred on diagrams. Show which clothing colours best suit the person through visual comparison. Keep text to a minimum and avoid paragraphs.

English transcription and description of the generated personal colour analysis. These are the image’s recommendations, which the author explicitly says he cannot assess in depth.

“Your palette”: undertone scale cool–neutral–warm, with the marker towards warm; depth light–medium–deep, with the marker towards deep; intensity soft–medium–high, with the marker around medium. Overall temperature: warm-neutral. A high-contrast black-and-white circle is selected among four contrast samples.

Flattering colours: portraits in dark teal, olive, rust, cream and navy. The fifteen swatches include cream, beige, tan, ochre-brown, dark brown, olive tones, green, teal, navy, plum and warm reds/oranges. Less flattering colours: portraits in pale pink, lilac, pale blue and grey; swatches in pale pink, lilac, blue-grey, pale blue, grey, muted blues, charcoal and black.

Ideal neutrals: cream, beige, taupe, brown and dark blue-grey. Metallics: gold and copper. Recommended patterns: leopard, paisley and houndstooth. Combinations that work: dark green/beige/ochre; navy/olive/cream; rust/cream/brown; teal/brown/ochre. Avoid very cool or neon outfits: the crossed-out combinations are neon pink/blue/yellow-green; pale aqua/lilac/pink; and pale grey/slate blue/navy.

As these examples show, ChatGPT Images 2.0 represents a significant leap in AI’s visual generation and editing capabilities; the leap is functional, not aesthetic. It still has limitations — for example, it still fails to depict certain Salamanca monuments accurately or place them correctly on a map — but it opens up a very promising field of experimentation for public, political and institutional communication. The underlying consequence is clear: the ability to produce high-quality visual communication ceases to be a bottleneck for small institutions, which may help balance communication against major political actors. The decisive question is no longer simply what the model can generate, but what responsible, clear and strategic uses we can design with it.
